The most operationally pressing question an auditor asks is also the one
conventional records answer worst: when a run stops unexpectedly, what was in
flight, and did it take effect? CORA's conductor makes this answerable by
construction. Because a setpoint and an action are side-effecting, the conductor
records a separate in-flight marker for each before the effect runs and the
outcome only after; a pure check, always safe to re-run, records a single
outcome. An interruption, whether a crash or a cancellation, therefore leaves a
characteristic trace: one in-flight marker with no matching outcome, at the step
that was executing when the conduct halted, because the marker is written before
the effect.

We reproduce that situation on a conducted run by simulating a crash after a
marker is written but before its outcome, and replay the truncated stream
through the scrubber (Figure~3). The fold reconstructs a state in which exactly
one activity is open, and the scrubber draws it as an open interval at its lane
and time, with the surrounding convergence bracket left unclosed. Rather than a
timeline that simply ends, the auditor sees precisely which step was mid-flight
and where (T1); dragging the cursor to the instant just before the gap shows the
last state known to be complete, the state a recovery would resume from. This is
the case post-hoc provenance graphs handle least gracefully: a graph assembled
after the fact has no node for an action that never reported a result, whereas
the intent-before-effect record, read by replay, makes the missing result itself
legible.
